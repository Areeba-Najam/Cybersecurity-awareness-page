01

Five threats worth knowing by name

Most attacks are one of these five. Learn the shape of each and the rest gets easier to spot.

Malware is any program built to damage, spy on, or take control of your device , viruses that ride inside files, worms that spread on their own, and trojans disguised as something useful.

Often arrives through: pirated downloads, infected attachments, or fake installers.

A type of malware that locks or encrypts your files, then demands payment for the key to get them back. It's spread hospitals, pipelines, and universities into full shutdowns in minutes.

Your best defence isn't paying , it's a backup you made before it happened.

An email, text, or call that impersonates someone you trust , your bank, your university, a coworker to get you to click a link, hand over a password, or send money.

See briefing 04 for the exact tells.

Attackers guess weak passwords, buy leaked ones from old breaches, or test stolen password lists against new sites , a technique called credential stuffing. It works because people reuse passwords.

One unique password per site closes this door entirely.

A company you have an account with gets broken into, and your email, password, or personal details end up copied, sold, or leaked. You didn't do anything wrong , but you still need to respond.

Check whether your email has appeared in a known breach, and change that password everywhere it was reused.

02

Five habits that do most of the work

None of these take more than a few minutes. Together they stop the overwhelming majority of real-world attacks.

  1. Turn on multi-factor authentication

    Add a second step , an app code or security key to your important accounts. It stops a stolen password from being enough on its own.

  2. Keep software updated

    Updates usually exist to close a specific hole attackers are already using. Turn on auto-update wherever you can, and don't postpone the rest.

  3. Back up what you can't lose

    Keep three copies of anything important, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy off-site or in the cloud. It's the one thing that makes ransomware a non-event.

  4. Treat public Wi-Fi as public

    Assume anything unencrypted on a coffee-shop network can be seen by someone else on it. Save banking and logins for trusted networks, or use a VPN.

  5. Pause before you click

    Urgency is a manufactured feeling. A message demanding immediate action is a reason to slow down, not speed up and hover the link, check the sender, verify another way.

03

Password safety, in plain terms

Length beats cleverness. A password manager beats memory.

Weak — guessed in seconds

Ali2003  ·  password1  ·  qwerty123

Short, personal, or a dictionary word and usually reused everywhere.

Strong , a passphrase

correct-harbor-lantern-42!

Four unrelated words plus a number are easy for you to recall and brutal for a machine to guess.

  • Aim for length over complexity. Fourteen characters or more; every extra character multiplies the effort needed to crack it.
  • Never reuse a password across sites. One leaked site shouldn't be able to unlock every account you own.
  • Use a password manager. Tools like Bitwarden or 1Password generate and store a unique password per site , you only remember one master password.
  • Don't store passwords in plain text. Not in a notes app, not in a spreadsheet, not on a sticky note on your monitor.
  • Change a password the moment a site tells you it was involved in a breach and change it anywhere else you'd reused it.
04

Phishing: the six red flags

Phishing doesn't hack your computer , it hacks your attention. Here's an annotated example.

From: IT-Support@paypa1-secure.com

Subject: URGENT: Your account will be CLOSED in 24h!!

Dear Costumer,

We detected unusuall login activity. You must verify you identety immediately or your account will be permanently suspended.

Click here to confirm: http://secure-paypa1.verify-login.ru/id

Send your password and card number to restore access.

Regards, The Security Team

  1. Look-alike sender domain "paypa1" with a numeral 1, not the real company domain.
  2. Manufactured urgency a countdown and threat designed to short-circuit careful thinking.
  3. Spelling and grammar errors "unusuall," "identety" real institutions proofread.
  4. Generic greeting "Dear Costumer" instead of your actual name.
  5. A link that doesn't match its destination hover before you click, and check where it really goes.
  6. A direct request for your password or card number no legitimate company will ever ask for this by email.

Tip: tap or hover a red flag on either side to see its match highlight.

05

Safe social media practices

Oversharing hands attackers the answers to your own security questions.

  • Lock down your privacy settings. Review who can see your posts, friends list, and contact details , don't leave it on public by default.
  • Think before posting real-time location. Save the vacation photos for after you're home.
  • Be wary of quizzes and "get to know me" posts. First pet, mother's maiden name, street you grew up on , these are also common security-question answers.
  • Verify before you trust a request. A friend's account asking for money or gift cards may be a cloned or hijacked profile.
  • Limit what a stranger could learn about your routine. Workplace, school, gym schedule , small details add up to a profile of you.
  • Use a different password for social accounts, with MFA on. A hijacked social account is often used to scam your own friends and followers.
06

If an account is hacked: what to do, in order

Move quickly, but move in this order , each step protects the ones after it.

  1. Regain access first

    Use the service's account-recovery flow immediately. If you're locked out, most platforms have a "forgot password" or "my account is compromised" path use it before anything else.

  2. Change the password and make it unique

    Set a new, strong password that isn't used anywhere else. If you'd reused the old one elsewhere, change it on every one of those sites too.

  3. Turn on multi-factor authentication

    If it wasn't already on, enable it now. This is the single step most likely to keep the account yours going forward.

  4. Check for damage

    Review recent activity, connected devices, forwarding rules (especially in email), and any new payment methods or app permissions the attacker may have added.

  5. Sign out other sessions

    Most platforms let you remotely log out every other active session. Use it to cut off any device the attacker is still using.

  6. Warn people who trust you

    Let contacts know the account was compromised, especially if scam messages may have gone out in your name.

  7. Report it

    Report the incident to the platform, and to your bank if payment details were exposed. In serious cases, file a report with your local cybercrime authority.
